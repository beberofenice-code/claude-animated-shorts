# "The Ghost Ship Mary Celeste" — original script v1 (2026-11-24)

## Facts (sources)
- Brigantine *Mary Celeste* left New York 7 Nov 1872 for Genoa with 1,701 barrels of (denatured) alcohol; aboard:
  Capt. Benjamin Briggs, his wife Sarah, their 2-year-old daughter Sophia, 7 crew (10 people). Last log entry 25 Nov,
  off the Azores. (Wikipedia "Mary Celeste"; Britannica; RMG "The mystery of the Mary Celeste")
- 4 Dec 1872 the *Dei Gratia* sighted her ~400 miles east of the Azores, sailing erratically under partial sail; nobody
  aboard; seaworthy; ~6 months of food and water; cargo intact; the single lifeboat (yawl) missing. (Wikipedia; Smithsonian; History.com)
- At unloading in Genoa, 9 barrels were empty; they were red oak, more porous than the white-oak rest. (Wikipedia; Smithsonian)
- A rope (the main peak halyard) was found trailing in the water behind the ship — read as a towline to the boat that
  parted. (American Heritage; Skeptoid #289)
- Alcohol-vapour theory: UCL chemist Andrea Sella's 2006 replica-hold test showed a vapour "pressure-wave" explosion that
  leaves no scorch marks (UCL news, May 2006). Still a theory — told as "the favourite theory".
- Jan 1884, Cornhill Magazine: anonymous story "J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement" by a 24-year-old doctor, Arthur Conan Doyle.
  It renamed the ship "Marie Celeste" (the spelling stuck) and put her boats still in place. Gibraltar investigator
  Solly-Flood called it "a fabrication from beginning to end". (Wikipedia "J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement"; Visit Portsmouth)

## Script (~241 words)
In 1872, a ship was found sailing across the Atlantic, perfectly seaworthy, with nobody on board.
She was the Mary Celeste, carrying alcohol from New York to Italy, with the captain, his wife, their two-year-old daughter, and seven crew.
A few weeks later, another ship spots her wobbling along like nobody's steering. Because nobody is.
The sailors climb aboard. Sails up. Six months of food and water. Cargo untouched.
The only things missing are the lifeboat, and all ten people.
Luckily, nothing was wrong with the ship. Unluckily, nothing was wrong with the ship, so nobody could explain why everyone left.
Here's the favourite theory. Nine of her seventeen hundred barrels were found empty, made of a leakier wood.
A hold full of alcohol fumes, with his wife and daughter aboard? The captain wasn't waiting to see what one spark would do.
So everyone climbs into the lifeboat, tied on with a long rope, just until the air clears.
It's like running outside when the smoke alarm goes off, and the front door slams shut behind you.
A frayed rope was found trailing in the water. The wind filled the sails, the rope snapped, and the ship sailed on without them.
They were never seen again.
Twelve years later, a young doctor wrote a made-up story about her, put the lifeboat back, and even got her name wrong.
Half the world still calls her the Marie Celeste. The doctor's name was Arthur Conan Doyle.
