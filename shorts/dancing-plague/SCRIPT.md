# "The Dancing Plague" — original script v1 (2026-11-23)
Format: ~60 s vertical short, ~3.7 words/s, one steady narrator tone, English only, no captions.

## Facts (sources)
- Strasbourg (then Holy Roman Empire), July 1518. A woman, Frau Troffea, began dancing alone in her street around
  14 July and kept going for about six days; her husband could not stop her.
- Within about a week ~34 others had joined; by August chroniclers put the number as high as ~400.
- Background: years of famine, failed harvests, soaring bread prices, smallpox, syphilis and leprosy; locals believed
  St Vitus could curse sinners with uncontrollable dancing.
- City physicians declared it a natural illness of "hot blood"; the cure was to dance it out. The council cleared two
  guildhalls, built a wooden stage near the horse and grain markets, hired pipers and drummers, and paid strong men
  to keep dancers upright.
- Dancers collapsed from exhaustion; some reportedly died (numbers disputed; the "15 a day" figure comes from a later
  chronicle and is not in contemporary records).
- The council reversed course: public music and dancing were banned, and the dancers were taken by wagon to the shrine
  of St Vitus at Saverne (Zabern) in the hills, where they were led around the altar and given small crosses and
  red shoes anointed with holy oil. The outbreak faded by early September.
- Modern explanation (historian John Waller): mass psychogenic illness — stress plus shared belief spreading
  behaviour person to person. Ergot poisoning is largely rejected (it causes convulsions, not days of dancing).
Sources: Wikipedia "Dancing plague of 1518"; John Waller, *A Time to Dance, a Time to Die* (2008) and
"A forgotten plague: making sense of dancing mania", The Lancet 373 (2009).

## Script
In 1518, hundreds of people danced in the streets for weeks, and nobody could make them stop.
It's summer in Strasbourg. The harvests have failed, bread costs a fortune, and everyone is exhausted.
One morning, a woman named Frau Troffea steps outside and starts dancing. No music. No reason.
Her husband begs her to stop. She dances for six days.
By the end of the week, thirty more people have joined her.
By August, there are hundreds, hopping and spinning in the summer heat.
So the city's doctors make a diagnosis: hot blood.
Luckily, the doctors had a cure.
Unluckily, the cure was more dancing.
The council clears two halls, builds a wooden stage, hires pipers and drummers, and pays strong men to hold the dancers up.
Then the dancers start collapsing.
You see, historians think it spread like a yawn.
Ever watched someone yawn and suddenly had to yawn too? Fear, hunger, and a local legend that Saint Vitus cursed sinners with dancing turned one dancer into a whole city.
So the council finally flips. Music is banned, and the dancers are carted up into the hills to the shrine of Saint Vitus.
And the cure they finally tried? A brand new pair of red shoes.
